[SLIDE CONTENT: Preprocessing Pipeline]
1. Feature Engineering:
   - Per-capita metrics (CO2_per_Capita, Energy_Consumption_Per_Capita)
   - Temporal lags (Renewable_Energy_Usage_lag1)
   - 16 engineered features total

2. Feature Selection:
   - Selected top 20 features by importance
   - Removed redundant/collinear features

3. Train/Test Split:
   - Train: 1900-2009 (20,131 observations)
   - Test: 2010-2023 (2,691 observations)
   - CRITICAL: Time-aware split to prevent data leakage

4. Scaling:
   - RobustScaler (fitted on training data only)
   - Handles outliers without leaking information

[SCRIPT]
"The most critical aspect of our preprocessing is the temporal split. 
We train only on historical data (1900-2009) and test on the held-out 
future period (2010-2023). This prevents any data leakage that would 
compromise our real-world evaluation."

   socioeconomic features alone cannot reliably predict temperature. 
   Physical climate drivers like ocean-atmosphere dynamics and radiative

[SCRIPT]
"Key insight: The drivers are fundamentally different for each target."

"For Temperature, urbanization is the top driver - but even the most 
important feature explains very little variance given the poor overall R²."

"For CO2 emissions, CO2_per_Capita and Population dominate, which aligns 
with established emission accounting frameworks (the Kaya identity)."

[SLIDE CONTENT: Marginal Effects Analysis]
- Urbanization → Temperature: NON-LINEAR THRESHOLD
  * Low-medium urbanization: neutral effect
  * Extreme urbanization (>90%): strong warming (+0.03°C)
  
- Population → CO2: NEAR-LINEAR MONOTONIC
  * Consistent positive relationship across all population levels
  * Each decile increase contributes ~40 Mt CO2

[SCRIPT]
"The marginal effects analysis reveals important patterns:

1. URBANIZATION-TEMPERATURE: Shows a threshold effect - warming only 
   becomes significant at extreme urbanization levels (>90%), likely 
   due to urban heat island effects and concentrated energy demand.

2. POPULATION-CO2: Shows a strong linear relationship - larger 
   populations consistently drive higher emissions across the entire 
   distribution, with no saturation effect."

[SCRIPT]

[SLIDE CONTENT: Key Takeaways]
1. CO2 emissions are highly predictable from socioeconomic features 
   (R² > 0.98) - validates strong driver-outcome relationships

2. Temperature anomaly prediction is challenging with socioeconomic 
   data alone (R² < 0) - physical climate variables needed

3. SHAP analysis reveals different driver patterns for each target:
   - Temperature: threshold effects from urbanization
   - CO2: linear scaling with population and economic activity

[SLIDE CONTENT: Limitations & Future Work]
- Temperature predictions require physical climate variables
- CO2 performance partly driven by feature engineering (per-capita metrics)
- Panel data structure could benefit from country-specific modeling

[SCRIPT]

This work provides a framework for understanding the differential 
predictability of climate outcomes."
