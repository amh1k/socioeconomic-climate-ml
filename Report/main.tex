\section{Introduction}
Global climate change poses unprecedented challenges to ecological stability, public health, and socioeconomic development. While traditional climate forecasting relies on complex physical simulation models, data-driven machine learning (ML) approaches have emerged as complementary tools for identifying macro-level patterns from historical observations. Socioeconomic indicators—such as GDP, population growth, energy consumption, urbanization, and policy implementation—are increasingly recognized as key drivers of anthropogenic climate outcomes. 


\textbf{Problem Statement:} Most existing studies treat emission trajectories and temperature anomalies as isolated prediction tasks, often neglecting temporal dependencies, multi-target interactions, and model interpretability. Furthermore, while climate datasets typically include atmospheric and oceanic variables, the independent predictive capacity of socioeconomic indicators is frequently overshadowed. This project deliberately centers socioeconomic drivers to quantify their primary contribution to climate forecasting, testing whether human activity metrics can reliably predict emission trajectories and thermal dynamics alongside environmental covariates.

\textbf{Research Objectives:} This project addresses these limitations by developing a rigorous, temporally validated ML pipeline that simultaneously predicts temperature anomalies and CO$_2$ emissions using panel data spanning 1900--2023. Specifically, we aim to:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=*]
    \item Engineer domain-informed temporal features and reduce dimensionality via importance-based selection.
    \item Train and compare Ridge Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM models.
    \item Optimize hyperparameters using Optuna with TimeSeriesSplit cross-validation.
    \item Interpret predictions using SHAP to identify target-specific drivers and non-linear marginal effects.
    \item Evaluate the scientific validity of socioeconomic-only forecasting for emissions versus temperature anomalies.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Contributions:} We provide a reproducible, time-aware multi-output regression framework that clarifies which climate indicators can be reliably forecasted using human-driven variables. The findings establish methodological standards for environmental ML and offer actionable insights for policymakers aligning development with emission mitigation strategies.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Machine Learning in Climate Science}
Recent advances in machine learning have transformed climate modeling, enabling data-driven approaches to complement traditional physical simulations. Comprehensive reviews highlight the growing role of deep learning and gradient-boosted ensembles in downscaling projections, detecting extreme events, and emulating complex Earth system components \cite{rolnick2022tackling}. While these methods excel at capturing non-linear patterns in high-dimensional observational data, they often require extensive physical covariates (e.g., sea surface temperatures, atmospheric pressure fields) that limit their direct application to macro-scale socioeconomic forecasting \cite{ipcc2023synthesis}.

\subsection{Socioeconomic Drivers of Carbon Emissions}
The relationship between human activity and greenhouse gas emissions is well-established in environmental economics. The Kaya identity decomposition demonstrates that CO$_2$ emissions scale deterministically with population, GDP per capita, energy intensity, and carbon intensity of energy supply \cite{friedlingstein2022global}. While traditional econometric models (e.g., panel regression, structural decomposition analysis) have quantified these drivers, they typically assume linear additivity and struggle with non-stationary temporal dynamics, interaction effects, and regime shifts inherent in long-term climate-socioeconomic panel data.

\subsection{Multi-Target Environmental Prediction}
Environmental systems are inherently multivariate, yet most predictive studies model individual indicators in isolation. Multi-output regression frameworks have gained traction in air quality, hydrology, and renewable energy forecasting, where correlated targets benefit from shared representation learning \cite{demir2021multi}. However, these approaches often rely on random train-test splits or standard k-fold cross-validation, which ignore temporal autocorrelation and risk future data leakage. Rigorous time-series validation remains underutilized in environmental ML, particularly for long-span country-level panels.

\subsection{Interpretability in Environmental ML}
As machine learning models grow in complexity, post-hoc interpretability methods have become essential for translating black-box predictions into actionable policy insights. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME provide model-agnostic feature attribution, enabling researchers to quantify marginal effects, detect non-linear thresholds, and validate driver importance against domain knowledge \cite{lundberg2017shap}. In climate and sustainability applications, these techniques have successfully disentangled confounding variables and highlighted context-dependent relationships, though their integration with temporally strict validation pipelines remains limited \cite{molnar2022interpretable}.

\subsection{Data Collection \& Preprocessing}
Country-year panel data spanning 1900--2023 was aggregated from publicly available climate and socioeconomic repositories. The primary dataset \cite{your_dataset_citation} includes temperature anomalies, CO$_2$ emissions, GDP, population, energy consumption, urbanization rates, policy scores, and environmental indicators at the country-year level. Missing values were imputed using training-period medians, and features were scaled using \texttt{RobustScaler} to mitigate outlier influence.
\subsection{Feature Engineering}
Base features will include per-capita economic metrics, renewable energy ratios, policy-economic interactions, and decade indicators. Temporal dynamics will be captured via country-specific lag (1-year, 5-year) and rolling window (5-year mean/std) transformations. Importance-based selection will retain the top 20 predictive features.

\subsection{Modeling Framework}
A \texttt{MultiOutputRegressor} wrapper will enable simultaneous prediction of \texttt{Temperature\_Anomaly} and \texttt{CO2\_Emissions}. Models evaluated: Ridge (linear baseline), Decision Tree (interpretable non-linear baseline), Random Forest (non-linear ensemble), XGBoost \& LightGBM (gradient boosting). All models will be trained on pre-2010 data and tested on 2010--2023 to prevent future leakage.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}
Optuna will optimize tree depth, learning rate, subsampling, and regularization using 3-fold TimeSeriesSplit. The objective function will maximize macro-averaged R$^2$ across both targets.

\subsection{Evaluation \& Interpretation}
Performance will be measured using RMSE, MAE, and R$^2$ per target and macro-averaged. SHAP (\texttt{TreeExplainer}) will generate global importance rankings, dependence plots, and marginal effect visualizations to explain feature contributions and interaction effects.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The pipeline will be modularized (\texttt{src/data\_loader.py}, \texttt{src/feature\_engineering.py}) and orchestrated via Jupyter notebooks. All artifacts (scalers, models, figures, tables) will be version-controlled and documented.

\section{Project Duration \& Execution Timeline}
The complete development, evaluation, and interpretation of this multi-target climate forecasting pipeline was conducted over a structured \textbf{12-week period}, organized into six iterative phases that align with the project methodology, experimental workflow, and reporting milestones:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Weeks 1--2 (Problem Definition \& Exploratory Analysis):} Literature synthesis, dataset acquisition, initial temporal trend visualization, and Pearson correlation analysis to identify the fundamental asymmetry between CO$_2$ and temperature predictability.
    \item \textbf{Weeks 3--4 (Preprocessing \& Feature Engineering):} Implementation of strict temporal validation (train: 1900--2009, test: 2010--2023), training-period median imputation, \texttt{RobustScaler} application, country-specific lag/rolling feature creation, and importance-based reduction to the top 20 socioeconomic indicators.
    \item \textbf{Weeks 5--6 (Model Implementation \& Baseline Evaluation):} Development of the \texttt{MultiOutputRegressor} framework, training of Ridge, Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM baselines, and initial macro-R$^2$ benchmarking.
    \item \textbf{Weeks 7--8 (Hyperparameter Optimization):} Integration of Optuna with 3-fold \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit} cross-validation, macro-averaged R$^2$ objective tuning, and selection of the final XGBoost configuration (\texttt{max\_depth=4}, \texttt{learning\_rate=0.048}, \texttt{subsample=0.625}).
    \item \textbf{Weeks 9--10 (Interpretation \& Validation):} SHAP-based global importance ranking, dependence and marginal effect plotting, and target-specific driver analysis to quantify the predictive boundary of socioeconomic-prioritized modeling.
    \item \textbf{Weeks 11--12 (Synthesis \& Reporting):} Comparative model analysis, accuracy-speed trade-off evaluation, limitation documentation, final figure/table generation, manuscript drafting, and reproducibility audit.
\end{itemize}

This phased execution ensured that temporal leakage was prevented at every stage, feature selection was driven by empirical importance rather than arbitrary thresholds, and interpretation was conducted separately for each target to respect their distinct physical and socioeconomic drivers. All code, serialized artifacts, and environment specifications were version-controlled throughout the duration to support transparency and open science standards.

\section{Experimental Results \& Evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}
Performance was assessed using three complementary metrics:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{RMSE} (Root Mean Squared Error): Penalizes large errors, sensitive to outliers
    \item \textbf{MAE} (Mean Absolute Error): Interpretable average error magnitude
    \item \textbf{R$^2$} (Coefficient of Determination): Proportion of variance explained relative to a mean baseline
\end{itemize}
Metrics were computed \textit{per target} and \textit{macro-averaged} across both targets for model ranking. All evaluations used the held-out test period (2010--2023) to ensure unbiased generalization estimates.

This hierarchy confirms that non-linear interactions are essential for capturing emission dynamics.

\text{CO}_2 = \text{Population} \times \frac{\text{GDP}}{\text{Population}} \times \frac{\text{Energy}}{\text{GDP}} \times \frac{\text{CO}_2}{\text{Energy}}
\]

\subsubsection{Temperature Anomaly: Fundamental Predictability Limit}
Negative R$^2$ across all architectures indicates that socioeconomic features alone cannot reliably forecast temperature anomalies. This is not a modeling failure but a scientific finding: temperature dynamics depend on physical processes (ocean circulation, radiative forcing, aerosol effects) not captured in socioeconomic indicators. % NEW:
The consistent RMSE $\approx$ 0.66$^\circ$C and uniformly negative R$^2$ (best: -0.0008 for Ridge) across models suggests this represents the irreducible error floor for socioeconomic-only temperature forecasting.

\section{Conclusion}
This study developed and evaluated a temporally validated multi-target machine learning pipeline to predict temperature anomalies and CO$_2$ emissions using a dataset encompassing both socioeconomic and environmental indicators. Although environmental covariates are present, our analysis prioritizes socioeconomic variables to evaluate their primary policy relevance. Our experiments demonstrate a fundamental asymmetry in predictability: CO$_2$ emissions are highly forecastable (R$^2$ $>$ 0.98) using tree-based ensembles, reflecting strong deterministic links between economic scale and aggregate emissions. In contrast, temperature anomalies consistently yield negative R$^2$ across all architectures, confirming that socioeconomic drivers, while highly effective for emissions, provide limited direct signal for temperature dynamics compared to physical climate factors.
SHAP-based interpretation reveals that CO$_2$ predictions are driven by population and per-capita economic metrics, while temperature associations remain weak and threshold-dependent (e.g., urbanization effects only emerge at $>$90th percentile). Hyperparameter tuning provided marginal gains, indicating that model architecture and temporal validation matter more than parameter optimization for this task. 

 \textbf{Limitations \& Future Work:} The negative temperature R$^2$ reflects the inherent predictive boundary of socioeconomic-prioritized modeling rather than a methodological flaw: accurate temperature forecasting requires stronger emphasis on environmental covariates. This study intentionally centers socioeconomic drivers to quantify their standalone utility, revealing that while they robustly track emission trajectories, they cannot substitute for atmospheric dynamics in temperature prediction. Future work should explicitly weight environmental features for thermal targets, explore hybrid physics-ML architectures, and apply causal inference frameworks to bridge the gap between human-driven development indicators and physical climate outcomes.
All code, trained models, and reproducibility artifacts are publicly available to support transparent, auditable environmental ML research.
