\begin{abstract}
Global climate change poses unprecedented challenges to ecological
stability, public health, and socioeconomic development. While traditional
climate forecasting relies on complex physical simulation models, data-driven
machine learning (ML) approaches have emerged as complementary tools for
identifying macro-level patterns from historical observations. This project
develops a temporally validated ML pipeline to simultaneously predict
temperature anomalies and CO\textsubscript{2} emissions using century-scale
country-level panel data that includes both socioeconomic and environmental
indicators. While environmental covariates are present, this study
deliberately prioritizes socioeconomic variables to evaluate their primary
policy relevance and predictive boundaries. By integrating advanced feature
engineering, gradient-boosted ensembles, hyperparameter optimization, and
SHAP-based interpretation, the research clarifies which climate indicators can
be reliably forecasted using human-driven variables, providing actionable
insights for policymakers aligning development with emission mitigation
strategies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Global climate change poses unprecedented challenges to ecological stability,
public health, and socioeconomic development. While traditional climate
forecasting relies on complex physical simulation models, data-driven ML
approaches have emerged as complementary tools for identifying macro-level
patterns from historical observations. Socioeconomic indicators---such as
GDP, population growth, energy consumption, urbanization, and policy
implementation---are increasingly recognized as key drivers of anthropogenic
climate outcomes.

\textit{Problem Statement:} Most existing studies treat emission trajectories
and temperature anomalies as isolated prediction tasks, often neglecting
temporal dependencies, multi-target interactions, and model interpretability.
Furthermore, while climate datasets typically include atmospheric and oceanic
variables, the independent predictive capacity of socioeconomic indicators is
frequently overshadowed. This project deliberately centers socioeconomic
drivers to quantify their primary contribution to climate forecasting, testing
whether human activity metrics can reliably predict emission trajectories and
thermal dynamics alongside environmental covariates.

\textit{Research Objectives:} This project addresses these limitations by
developing a rigorous, temporally validated ML pipeline that simultaneously
predicts temperature anomalies and CO\textsubscript{2} emissions using panel
data spanning 1900--2023. Specifically, we aim to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Engineer domain-informed temporal features and reduce dimensionality
    via importance-based selection.
    \item Train and compare Ridge Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest,
    XGBoost, and LightGBM models.
    \item Optimize hyperparameters using Optuna with TimeSeriesSplit
    cross-validation.
    \item Interpret predictions using SHAP to identify target-specific drivers
    and non-linear marginal effects.
    \item Evaluate the scientific validity of socioeconomic-only forecasting
    for emissions versus temperature anomalies.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Contributions:} We provide a reproducible, time-aware multi-output
regression framework that clarifies which climate indicators can be reliably
forecasted using human-driven variables. The findings establish methodological
standards for environmental ML and offer actionable insights for policymakers
aligning development with emission mitigation strategies.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Machine Learning in Climate Science}

Recent advances in machine learning have transformed climate modeling,
enabling data-driven approaches to complement traditional physical simulations.
Comprehensive reviews highlight the growing role of deep learning and
gradient-boosted ensembles in downscaling projections, detecting extreme
events, and emulating complex Earth system components \cite{rolnick2022tackling}.
While these methods excel at capturing non-linear patterns in
high-dimensional observational data, they often require extensive physical
covariates (e.g., sea surface temperatures, atmospheric pressure fields) that
limit their direct application to macro-scale socioeconomic
forecasting \cite{ipcc2023synthesis}.

\subsection{Socioeconomic Drivers of Carbon Emissions}

The relationship between human activity and greenhouse gas emissions is
well-established in environmental economics. The Kaya identity decomposition
demonstrates that CO\textsubscript{2} emissions scale deterministically with
population, GDP per capita, energy intensity, and carbon intensity of energy
supply \cite{friedlingstein2022global}. While traditional econometric models
(e.g., panel regression, structural decomposition analysis) have quantified
these drivers, they typically assume linear additivity and struggle with
non-stationary temporal dynamics, interaction effects, and regime shifts
inherent in long-term climate-socioeconomic panel data.

\subsection{Multi-Target Environmental Prediction}

Environmental systems are inherently multivariate, yet most predictive studies
model individual indicators in isolation. Multi-output regression frameworks
have gained traction in air quality, hydrology, and renewable energy
forecasting, where correlated targets benefit from shared representation
learning \cite{demir2021multi}. However, these approaches often rely on random
train-test splits or standard $k$-fold cross-validation, which ignore temporal
autocorrelation and risk future data leakage. Rigorous time-series validation
remains underutilized in environmental ML, particularly for long-span
country-level panels.

\subsection{Interpretability in Environmental ML}

As machine learning models grow in complexity, post-hoc interpretability
methods have become essential for translating black-box predictions into
actionable policy insights. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME
provide model-agnostic feature attribution, enabling researchers to quantify
marginal effects, detect non-linear thresholds, and validate driver importance
against domain knowledge \cite{lundberg2017shap}. In climate and sustainability
applications, these techniques have successfully disentangled confounding
variables and highlighted context-dependent relationships, though their
integration with temporally strict validation pipelines remains
limited \cite{molnar2022interpretable}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis}

We conducted comprehensive exploratory data analysis to understand temporal patterns, 
feature distributions, target variable characteristics, and inter-feature relationships, 
which directly informed our modeling strategy and preprocessing decisions.

\subsubsection{Temporal Trends}

Figure~\ref{fig:climate_trends} shows historical trends of Temperature Anomaly and 
CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions (1900--2023). Both indicators exhibit high interannual 
variability with no smooth monotonic trend, justifying our choice of temporal validation 
(train: 1900--2009, test: 2010--2023) rather than random splitting to prevent future 
data leakage.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/eda_global_trends.png}
    \caption{Historical trends of Temperature Anomaly ($^\circ$C, red) and 
    CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions (Mt, blue) from 1900 to 2023. The vertical dashed 
    line at 2010 indicates the train/test split. High year-to-year variability 
    necessitates temporal feature engineering.}
    \label{fig:climate_trends}
\end{figure}

Notably, the time series reveals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Temperature Anomaly}: Fluctuates around zero with increasing volatility 
    in recent decades, reflecting both natural variability and anthropogenic forcing.
    \item \textbf{CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions}: Shows gradual increase from 
    $\approx 4.7 \times 10^8$ Mt (1900) to $\approx 5.2 \times 10^8$ Mt (2023), with 
    notable dips corresponding to economic recessions (1920s, 1970s oil crisis, 2008 
    financial crisis).
    \item \textbf{Decoupling}: The two series do not exhibit perfect co-movement, 
    suggesting that emission trajectories and temperature responses operate on different 
    temporal scales and are mediated by distinct physical and socioeconomic processes.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Target Variable Distributions}

Understanding the distributional characteristics of our prediction targets is critical 
for model selection and evaluation metric choice.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/eda_target_distributions.png}
    \caption{Distribution of target variables. Left: Temperature Anomaly shows 
    approximately normal distribution centered near zero (mean: 0.001$^\circ$C, 
    median: -0.001$^\circ$C). Right: CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions exhibits 
    right-skewed distribution (mean: $5.00 \times 10^8$ Mt, median: 
    $5.00 \times 10^8$ Mt) requiring log-transformation for modeling.}
    \label{fig:target_distributions}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Temperature Anomaly} (Fig.~\ref{fig:target_distributions}, left) exhibits an 
approximately normal distribution centered at zero (mean: 0.001$^\circ$C, median: 
-0.001$^\circ$C), with range [-2.0, 2.0]$^\circ$C. This symmetric distribution supports 
the use of mean-squared error-based metrics and suggests no systematic bias in the 
anomaly calculation.

\textbf{CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions} (Fig.~\ref{fig:target_distributions}, right) 
displays a right-skewed distribution with mean $5.00 \times 10^8$ Mt and median 
$5.00 \times 10^8$ Mt, spanning several orders of magnitude ($10^5$ to $10^{10}$ Mt). 
This extreme skewness motivated our use of \texttt{RobustScaler} (which uses median 
and interquartile range) rather than standard normalization, and suggests that 
log-transformation may improve model performance for this target.

\subsubsection{Feature Distribution Analysis}

We examined the distribution of key socioeconomic and environmental features to 
identify preprocessing requirements and potential modeling challenges.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/eda_numerica_features_distribution.png}
    \caption{Distribution of Waste\_Management feature showing approximately normal 
    distribution (mean: $\approx 50\%$, std: $\approx 20\%$) with slight left skew, 
    indicating most countries have moderate waste management infrastructure.}
    \label{fig:waste_dist}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:waste_dist} shows the distribution of the Waste\_Management feature, 
which exhibits an approximately normal distribution centered around 50\% with standard 
deviation of $\approx 20\%$. The slight left skew suggests that most countries have 
moderate waste management infrastructure, with fewer observations at extreme low or 
high values. This well-behaved distribution requires no special transformation beyond 
standard scaling.

Similar analyses were conducted for all 20 selected features, revealing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Economic indicators} (GDP, GDP per capita): Highly right-skewed, 
    requiring log-transformation
    \item \textbf{Policy scores}: Approximately uniform distribution, indicating 
    diverse policy implementation across countries and time periods
    \item \textbf{Energy metrics} (Renewable\_Energy\_Usage, Fossil\_Fuel\_Usage): 
    Bimodal distributions reflecting the global energy transition
    \item \textbf{Environmental indicators}: Mixed distributions, some requiring 
    Box-Cox transformation for normality
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Feature-Target Relationships}

Pearson correlation analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:target_correlations}) reveals a critical 
asymmetry between the two targets:

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/eda_target_correlations_both.png}
    \caption{Feature correlations with Temperature Anomaly (left) and CO\textsubscript{2} 
    Emissions (right). All correlations with Temperature are negligible ($|r| < 0.02$), 
    while CO\textsubscript{2} shows modest linear associations.}
    \label{fig:target_correlations}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Temperature Anomaly}: All features show negligible linear correlations 
($|r| < 0.02$), indicating that socioeconomic indicators alone lack sufficient signal 
for reliable temperature forecasting. This explains why all models achieve $R^2 < 0$ 
for this target.

\textbf{CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions}: Modest but non-zero correlations 
($|r| \approx 0.01$--$0.02$) suggest that non-linear models can extract predictive 
signal from socioeconomic features, consistent with established emission accounting 
frameworks.

\subsubsection{Bivariate Relationships: GDP vs CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions}

To visualize the relationship between economic activity and emissions, we plotted 
GDP against CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions (Figure~\ref{fig:gdp_co2}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/eda_gdp_co2_simple.png}
    \caption{Scatter plot of GDP vs CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions showing weak linear 
    relationship (Pearson $r = -0.000$). The red line indicates the linear fit. 
    High scatter suggests that GDP alone is insufficient to predict emissions, 
    necessitating multi-feature modeling.}
    \label{fig:gdp_co2}
\end{figure}

Surprisingly, the scatter plot reveals virtually no linear correlation 
(Pearson $r = -0.000$) between GDP and CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions at the 
country-year level. This counterintuitive finding has important implications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-linear relationship}: The GDP-emissions relationship is likely 
    non-linear, potentially following an Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) pattern 
    where emissions initially rise with economic growth but eventually decline after 
    reaching a certain development threshold.
    
    \item \textbf{Confounding factors}: Energy mix, policy interventions, industrial 
    structure, and technological efficiency moderate the GDP-emissions relationship, 
    necessitating multi-feature modeling rather than simple univariate regression.
    
    \item \textbf{Temporal dynamics}: The relationship may vary across time periods 
    and development stages, justifying our inclusion of temporal features (lags, 
    rolling statistics, decade indicators).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Summary of EDA Insights}

The exploratory analysis yields five critical insights that directly inform our 
methodology:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal dynamics are essential}: High interannual variability and 
    non-stationarity necessitate time-aware validation and temporal feature engineering 
    (lags, rolling statistics).
    
    \item \textbf{Target asymmetry}: Temperature and CO\textsubscript{2} exhibit 
    fundamentally different relationships with socioeconomic features, justifying 
    separate interpretation and target-specific analysis rather than aggregated 
    evaluation.
    
    \item \textbf{Distributional considerations}: CO\textsubscript{2} emissions require 
    robust scaling and potentially log-transformation due to extreme skewness, while 
    temperature anomalies are approximately normal.
    
    \item \textbf{Feature engineering is critical}: Raw features alone are insufficient; 
    derived features (per-capita metrics, energy ratios, policy interactions) and 
    temporal transformations are necessary to extract predictive signal.
    
    \item \textbf{Multicollinearity is manageable}: Moderate correlations between 
    economic indicators do not preclude multi-feature modeling, but justify the use 
    of regularization (Ridge/Lasso) to stabilize coefficient estimates.
\end{enumerate}

These findings validate our methodological choices: strict temporal validation, 
non-linear tree-based models, importance-based feature selection, and target-specific 
SHAP interpretation.
\subsection{Data Collection and Preprocessing}

Country-year panel data spanning 1900--2023 was aggregated from publicly
available climate and socioeconomic repositories. The primary dataset
\cite{ankit2025dataset} includes temperature anomalies, CO\textsubscript{2}
emissions, GDP, population, energy consumption, urbanization rates, policy
scores, and environmental indicators at the country-year level. Missing values
were imputed using training-period medians, and features were scaled using
\texttt{RobustScaler} to mitigate outlier influence.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}

Base features include per-capita economic metrics, renewable energy ratios,
policy-economic interactions, and decade indicators. Temporal dynamics are
captured via country-specific lag (1-year, 5-year) and rolling window (5-year
mean/std) transformations. Importance-based selection retains the top 20
predictive features.

\subsection{Modeling Framework}

A \texttt{MultiOutputRegressor} wrapper enables simultaneous prediction of
\texttt{Temperature\_Anomaly} and \texttt{CO2\_Emissions}. Models evaluated:
Ridge (linear baseline), Decision Tree (interpretable non-linear baseline),
Random Forest (non-linear ensemble), XGBoost and LightGBM (gradient boosting).
All models are trained on pre-2010 data and tested on 2010--2023 to prevent
future leakage.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}

Optuna optimizes tree depth, learning rate, subsampling, and regularization
using 3-fold \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}. The objective function maximizes
macro-averaged $R^2$ across both targets.

\subsection{Evaluation and Interpretation}

Performance is measured using RMSE, MAE, and $R^2$ per target and
macro-averaged. SHAP (\texttt{TreeExplainer}) generates global importance
rankings, dependence plots, and marginal effect visualizations to explain
feature contributions and interaction effects.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The pipeline is modularized (\texttt{src/data\_loader.py},
\texttt{src/feature\_engineering.py}) and orchestrated via Jupyter notebooks.
All artifacts (scalers, models, figures, tables) are version-controlled and
documented.

\section{Experimental Results and Evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}

Performance was assessed using three complementary metrics: RMSE (Root Mean
Squared Error), which penalizes large errors; MAE (Mean Absolute Error), an
interpretable average error magnitude; and $R^2$ (Coefficient of
Determination), which measures proportion of variance explained relative to a
mean baseline. Metrics were computed per target and macro-averaged across both
targets. All evaluations used the held-out test period (2010--2023).

Four key insights emerge from the comparative analysis:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-linearity is essential for CO\textsubscript{2}}: $R^2$
    jumps from 0.005 (Ridge) to $>0.98$ (ensembles). The Decision Tree's
    intermediate $R^2 = 0.819$ confirms that non-linearity alone provides
    substantial gains even without ensemble averaging.
    \item \textbf{Tuning provides marginal gains}: Optuna improved macro $R^2$
    from 0.311 to 0.325 for XGBoost, suggesting default parameters were
    already near-optimal. The primary benefit was stabilizing generalization.
    \item \textbf{Speed vs.\ accuracy}: Random Forest achieves the highest

    \item \textbf{Target asymmetry is fundamental}: No model achieves positive
    $R^2$ for Temperature, confirming that socioeconomic indicators inherently
    carry weaker predictive signal for thermal dynamics. This asymmetry
    justifies target-specific rather than aggregated evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Target-Specific Model Behavior}

\subsubsection{CO\textsubscript{2} Emissions: Deterministic Predictability}

Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM converge on near-perfect
CO\textsubscript{2} prediction ($R^2 > 0.98$), consistent with the Kaya
identity framework:
\[
\text{CO}_2 = \text{Pop.} \times \frac{\text{GDP}}{\text{Pop.}} \times
\frac{\text{Energy}}{\text{GDP}} \times \frac{\text{CO}_2}{\text{Energy}}
\]

\subsubsection{Temperature Anomaly: Fundamental Predictability Limit}

Negative $R^2$ across all architectures indicates that socioeconomic features
alone cannot reliably forecast temperature anomalies. This is a scientific
finding, not a modeling failure: temperature dynamics depend on physical
processes (ocean circulation, radiative forcing, aerosol effects) not captured
in socioeconomic indicators. The consistent RMSE $\approx 0.66^\circ$C and
uniformly negative $R^2$ (best: $-0.0008$ for Ridge) represents the
irreducible error floor for socioeconomic-only temperature forecasting.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

For emission forecasting, XGBoost or LightGBM offer the optimal
accuracy-speed balance. The Decision Tree provides a transparent, auditable
alternative ($R^2 = 0.819$) when interpretability is prioritized. For
temperature modeling, future work should explicitly weight environmental
covariates or adopt hybrid physics-ML architectures. For policy interpretation,
SHAP dependence plots reveal that urbanization and policy metrics matter most
at extreme deciles, suggesting targeted interventions (e.g., green
infrastructure in hyper-urbanized regions) rather than uniform national
strategies.

\section{Limitations and Ethical Considerations}

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}

While our temporally strict validation prevents future leakage, several limitations warrant acknowledgment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feature scope}: Although environmental covariates are present in the source dataset, our socioeconomic-prioritized feature selection may omit critical physical drivers (e.g., ocean heat content, aerosol optical depth) essential for temperature forecasting.
    \item \textbf{Temporal non-stationarity}: The post-2010 test period includes unprecedented climate events (e.g., 2015--2016 El Niño, pandemic-related emission shifts). Models trained on pre-2010 data may not generalize to novel regimes.
    \item \textbf{Country-level aggregation}: National averages mask subnational heterogeneity. Urban heat islands, regional policy variations, and localized emission sources are not resolved.
    \item \textbf{Causal interpretation}: SHAP values quantify predictive association, not causation. Observed driver--target relationships may reflect confounding or reverse causality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ethical and Policy Implications}

Our findings carry important implications for climate policy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emission forecasting}: The high predictability of CO\textsubscript{2} from socioeconomic indicators supports using ML for scenario planning and policy evaluation. However, models should be audited for bias against developing nations whose emission trajectories may differ from historical patterns.
    \item \textbf{Temperature forecasting}: The inability of socioeconomic-only models to forecast temperature underscores that mitigation policies must address physical climate processes, not just human activity metrics.
    \item \textbf{Equity considerations}: Feature importance rankings (e.g., GDP, Population) may inadvertently reinforce narratives that place disproportionate responsibility on populous or developing regions. Interpretability tools must be paired with contextual, justice-oriented analysis.
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This study developed and evaluated a temporally validated multi-target ML
pipeline to predict temperature anomalies and CO\textsubscript{2} emissions
using a dataset encompassing both socioeconomic and environmental indicators.
Our experiments demonstrate a fundamental asymmetry in predictability:
CO\textsubscript{2} emissions are highly forecastable ($R^2 > 0.98$) using
tree-based ensembles, reflecting strong deterministic links between economic
scale and aggregate emissions. In contrast, temperature anomalies consistently
yield negative $R^2$ across all architectures, confirming that socioeconomic
drivers provide limited direct signal for thermal dynamics.

SHAP-based interpretation reveals that CO\textsubscript{2} predictions are
driven by population and per-capita economic metrics, while temperature
associations remain weak and threshold-dependent (urbanization effects emerge
only above the 90th percentile). Hyperparameter tuning provided marginal gains,
indicating that model architecture and temporal validation matter more than
parameter optimization.

The negative temperature $R^2$ reflects an inherent predictive boundary rather
than a methodological flaw. Future work should explicitly weight environmental
features for thermal targets, explore hybrid physics-ML architectures, and
apply causal inference frameworks to bridge the gap between human-driven
development indicators and physical climate outcomes. All code, trained models,
and reproducibility artifacts are publicly available.
