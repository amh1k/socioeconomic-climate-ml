NOTEBOOK 1

Exploratory Data Analysis — 01_eda_visualizations.ipynb

0:20 – 0:55
We begin with Notebook 1 — Exploratory Data Analysis. The goal here is to understand the data's structure, assess its quality, tackle panel-balancing issues, and extract early insights about our two targets: Temperature Anomaly and CO₂ Emissions.
Cell 2 — Imports & Configuration
We load pandas, NumPy, seaborn, and scipy. Plotting styles are configured here — this cell is the environment setup ensuring all visualizations are consistent and reproducible.
Cell 3 — Data Load & Summary Statistics
This is our first look at the raw data. We load the dataset and immediately print: shape (rows × columns), data types, a full df.describe() statistical summary, missing value counts per column, and the number of unique countries and years.

Say: "At this point we have roughly 100,000 raw rows across 26 columns. The describe() output immediately flags skewed distributions — CO₂ emissions has a much larger std than mean, indicating heavy right skew from industrial outliers."
Cell 4 — Panel Integrity Check (Duplicates & Completeness)
This is where we tackle panel-balancing. We check for duplicate (Country, Year) pairs and compute panel completeness: Expected rows = 195 countries × 124 years = 24,180. Actual rows may differ — exposing gaps in historical coverage.

Say: "We discovered duplicate (Country, Year) entries — multiple records per country per year — a typical issue when merging datasets from different sources. We also found that the panel was unbalanced: not every country has data for every year, especially pre-1950."
Cell 5 — Records Per Group Distribution
We group by (Country, Year) and count how many input rows exist per group. The value_counts() output shows the frequency of duplicates — e.g., how many groups had 1 record vs. 2 vs. 3+.

Say: "Most groups had exactly 1 record; a subset had 2–3 due to source overlaps. This directly informed our aggregation strategy."
Cell 6 — Aggregation to Balance the Panel
We resolve duplicates here. All numeric columns are aggregated by taking the mean per (Country, Year) group, collapsing multiples into a single canonical row. After aggregation, the dataset becomes a clean, one-row-per-country-year panel.

Say: "By groupby-mean aggregation, we reduced the dataset from ~100K rows to ~23,797 rows — one row per (Country, Year) — giving us a balanced, structured panel ready for time-series modeling."
Cell 7 — Global Trends Over Time
A dual-axis time series plot: left axis shows Temperature Anomaly, right axis shows CO₂ Emissions. Overlaid with GDP and Population trends.

Say: "The diverging trends after 1950 are unmistakable — rapid industrial growth drives exponential CO₂ rise, while temperature anomaly climbs more gradually. This temporal pattern motivates the need for time-aware feature engineering."
Cell 11 — Full Correlation Heatmap
A heatmap of the full numeric correlation matrix. GDP, fossil fuel usage, population, and urbanization show strong positive correlations with CO₂. Temperature anomaly shows much weaker linear relationships with all features.
Cell 14 — EDA Summary & Key Findings
A printed structured summary confirming: final panel size (~23,797 rows), year range, country count, target statistics, and recommendations carried forward.

Say: "The EDA gave us two critical take-aways: CO₂ is strongly linearly predictable from socioeconomic data, while temperature anomaly is not — a finding our models would later confirm."
NOTEBOOK 2

Preprocessing Pipeline — 02_preprocessing_pipeline.ipynb

0:55 – 1:25
Notebook 2 is our preprocessing pipeline — converting the cleaned EDA output into a modeling-ready format without any data leakage.
Cell 2 — Imports & Directory Setup
We import pandas, sklearn's RobustScaler and TimeSeriesSplit, and joblib for serialization. Output directories for processed data and models are created here.

Say: "We chose RobustScaler specifically because economic data like GDP has extreme outliers — it scales using the interquartile range, making it outlier-resistant."
Cell 3 — Load Data & Temporal Train/Test Split
We load the cleaned CSV (Shape: 23,797 × 26). A temporal split is applied: Train = 1900–2009 (21,106 rows), Test = 2010–2023 (2,691 rows). No random shuffling allowed — this is a time series panel.

Say: "A strict temporal split is critical. If we randomly split, future data leaks into training — like predicting 1950 emissions using 2010 data. We train on the past and test on the most recent 14 years."
Cell 6 — Full Feature Engineering + Scaling + Save
The core preprocessing cell. Calls engineer_full_pipeline() from src/feature_engineering.py which generates 16 temporal features: lag variables, rolling means, energy intensity ratios, per-capita indicators, and policy-GDP interaction terms. Dataset grows from 26 → 47 columns. RobustScaler is fit on train only and applied to both train and test. Final artifacts saved: 4 CSV files + scaler pickle. Final shape: Train (20,131 × 20), Test (2,691 × 20).

Say: "All transformations — scaling, imputation, encoding — are fit on training data only and then applied to the test set. This is the gold standard for preventing leakage in time-series modeling."
⚠️ Key Insight to Mention: The scaler is saved as a pickle so any future inference on new data uses the exact same transformation fitted on 1900–2009 data — ensuring full reproducibility.
NOTEBOOK 3

Model Training & Tuning — 03_model_training_tuning.ipynb

1:25 – 2:05
Notebook 3 is where we train and compare five different ML architectures for multi-target regression — simultaneously predicting both Temperature Anomaly and CO₂ Emissions.
Cell 1 — Markdown: Objective
States the modeling objective: multi-target regression on two targets using MultiOutputRegressor wrappers and native multi-output models.
Cell 4 — Evaluation Function
evaluate_multi_target() computes R², RMSE, and MAE for each target independently and as a macro-average. This function is used consistently across all models for fair comparison.

Say: "Having a single evaluation harness ensures apples-to-apples comparison — the same metrics computed the same way for every model."
Cell 5 — Random Forest Training
200 trees, n_jobs=-1 (all CPU cores), random_state=42. Achieves near-perfect CO₂ R² ≈ 0.99, but lower Temperature Anomaly R².

Say: "Random Forest is our first strong baseline. The CO₂ performance is striking — almost perfect. Temperature anomaly is much harder to predict, which immediately tells us something scientifically important."
Cell 7 — XGBoost Training
200 estimators, max_depth=4, learning_rate=0.1, subsample=0.8. Comparable to Random Forest accuracy but significantly faster training time.
Cell 9 — LightGBM Training
Same config as XGBoost. Even faster thanks to histogram-based leaf-wise tree growth. GPU-compatible architecture.

Say: "We benchmarked three gradient-boosted tree models. All converge on similar accuracy — what differs is training speed and interpretability tradeoffs."
Cell 11 — Optuna Hyperparameter Tuning
Bayesian optimization with Optuna over the XGBoost search space for n_estimators, max_depth, learning_rate, subsample, colsample_bytree. Uses TimeSeriesSplit cross-validation as the inner loop to prevent leakage during tuning.

Say: "Optuna is smarter than grid search — it learns which hyperparameter regions are promising and focuses trials there. We ran N trials and found the globally optimal XGBoost configuration."
Cell 13 — Final Model Comparison Chart
Bar chart comparing all models on R², RMSE per target. XGBoost Tuned wins on the macro-average. The chart clearly shows the CO₂ vs Temperature Anomaly performance gap.

Say: "This summary chart is the key deliverable of Notebook 3. CO₂ R² is near 1.0 across all models. Temperature Anomaly R² is consistently lower — confirming the predictability asymmetry hypothesis."
NOTEBOOK 4

Model Interpretation — 04_interpretation.ipynb

2:05 – 2:40
Notebook 4 goes beyond accuracy — we use SHAP, SHapley Additive exPlanations, to understand WHY our best model makes specific predictions, and which socioeconomic factors matter most for each climate target.
Cell 2 — Load Best Model & SHAP Setup
Loads the saved XGBoost Tuned model from disk (joblib), loads the test set, and imports the SHAP library. Two separate models are loaded — one per target — since we wrapped with MultiOutputRegressor.
Cell 3 — Compute SHAP Values
shap.TreeExplainer is called for both the Temperature Anomaly and CO₂ Emissions sub-models. SHAP values are computed on the entire test set.

Say: "TreeExplainer is exact — not approximate — for tree-based models. It computes the contribution of every single feature to every single prediction in polynomial time."
Cell 4 — Global SHAP Summary Plots
get_feature_importance() computes mean absolute SHAP values per feature. Two SHAP beeswarm/bar plots are generated — one for each target — ranking features by global importance.

Say: "For CO₂: fossil fuel usage, population, and GDP dominate. For Temperature Anomaly: urbanization and lagged climate signals emerge. This asymmetry in driver structure is the paper's central finding."
Cell 5 — SHAP Dependence Plots
Marginal effect plots for the top drivers. Urbanization vs Temperature Anomaly shows a non-linear threshold — once urbanization crosses a certain level, its climate impact spikes disproportionately.

Say: "These dependence plots reveal threshold effects — non-linearities that a linear regression would completely miss. This is the real power of interpretable ML: quantifying when economic growth starts having disproportionate climate impact."
Cell 7 — Final Interpretation Summary
Printed output: CO₂ R² ≈ 0.99+, Temperature Anomaly R² significantly lower. Confirms the predictability asymmetry. Lists top 5 drivers per target.

Say: "The model summary confirms what the SHAP plots showed — socioeconomic data gives us a near-perfect lens into emissions, but temperature anomaly is governed by complex physical feedbacks that economics alone cannot capture."
